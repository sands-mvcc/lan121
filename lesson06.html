Network Operations II — Management, Monitoring & Disaster Recovery

Lesson 5 covered the services a network provides; this lesson covers how professionals keep the network itself alive. You'll build the documentation that makes a network knowable — physical and logical diagrams, rack elevations, cable maps, asset inventories, SLAs, and wireless surveys; manage the lifecycle from purchase to decommissioning with firmware, patching, and formal change management; protect configurations with baselines and understand exactly how full, incremental, differential, and snapshot backups differ; monitor with SNMP, flow data, packet capture, port mirroring, syslog aggregation, and APIs; plan for disaster with RPO/RTO, cold/warm/hot sites, and failover testing; and reach it all remotely through VPNs — with an interactive simulator — jump boxes, and out-of-band management. Exam 3 closes out Domain 3.

🎯

Learning Objectives

  1. Differentiate the documentation artifacts of a managed network — physical vs. logical diagrams, rack diagrams, cable maps, asset inventory, SLAs, and wireless surveys — and what each is used for. BLOOM: UNDERSTAND
  2. Explain lifecycle management (EOL/EOS, decommissioning, firmware and patch management) and walk through the stages of a formal change management process. BLOOM: UNDERSTAND
  3. Compare full, incremental, differential, and snapshot backups — what each captures, restore effort, and when each is the right choice. BLOOM: ANALYZE
  4. Select the appropriate monitoring technology — SNMP polls vs. traps, flow data, packet capture with port mirroring, syslog aggregation, baselines, or APIs — for a given visibility question. BLOOM: EVALUATE
  5. Apply RPO and RTO to choose among cold, warm, and hot recovery sites and between active-active and active-passive designs, and describe tabletop and failover testing. BLOOM: APPLY
  6. Contrast site-to-site and client-to-site VPNs and predict how traffic flows with full versus split tunneling. BLOOM: ANALYZE
  7. Describe secure administrative access using jump boxes and in-band vs. out-of-band management. BLOOM: UNDERSTAND

Key Terms & Concepts

🤝SLATAP TO FLIP
Service Level Agreement — the contract defining measurable expectations: uptime percentage, response time, repair time, and penalties when they're missed.
🪦EOL / EOSTAP TO FLIP
End of Life: the vendor stops selling the product. End of Support: the vendor stops patching it — running EOS gear means unfixable vulnerabilities.
📋Change ManagementTAP TO FLIP
The formal process for altering production: request → approval (CAB) → maintenance window → implementation with a tested rollback plan → documentation.
📏BaselineTAP TO FLIP
A recorded "normal" — either a golden configuration to compare against, or normal performance metrics (CPU, bandwidth, latency) so anomalies stand out.
🧩Incremental BackupTAP TO FLIP
Copies only what changed since the LAST BACKUP OF ANY TYPE. Fastest to run, smallest to store — but restoring needs the full plus every incremental since.
📚Differential BackupTAP TO FLIP
Copies everything changed since the LAST FULL backup — each one grows through the week. Restore = full + newest differential, just two sets.
📸SnapshotTAP TO FLIP
A point-in-time image of a VM or volume, captured in seconds at the storage layer. Perfect before risky changes — but it lives with the system, so it's not a true backup.
🚨SNMP TrapTAP TO FLIP
An UNSOLICITED alert a device pushes to the management station the moment something happens (interface down, PSU failed) — versus polling, where the NMS asks on a schedule.
🌊NetFlowTAP TO FLIP
Flow data — per-conversation metadata (who talked to whom, ports, bytes, duration) exported by routers/switches. The phone bill of the network: patterns without payloads.
🪵SyslogTAP TO FLIP
The standard for shipping device logs (UDP 514) to a central collector, with severity levels 0 (emergency) through 7 (debug). The raw feed for log aggregation and SIEMs.
🪞Port MirroringTAP TO FLIP
SPAN — the switch copies traffic from chosen ports/VLANs to an analyzer port so packet capture can see conversations that switching would normally hide.
⏪RPOTAP TO FLIP
Recovery Point Objective — how much DATA you can afford to lose, measured backward in time from the disaster to the last good backup/replica.
⏩RTOTAP TO FLIP
Recovery Time Objective — how much DOWNTIME you can afford, measured forward from the disaster until service is restored.
🔥Hot SiteTAP TO FLIP
A fully equipped duplicate facility with near-real-time data replication — take over in minutes. Costs the most; cold (empty room) and warm (gear, stale data) trade money for time.
🔀Split TunnelTAP TO FLIP
VPN mode where only corporate-bound traffic uses the encrypted tunnel; everything else exits locally. Saves bandwidth, but general browsing bypasses HQ inspection.
🏃Jump BoxTAP TO FLIP
A hardened, logged intermediary host — admins connect to it, and only it may reach the management network. One controlled, audited doorway instead of many.
🚑OOB ManagementTAP TO FLIP
Out-of-band — a separate physical path to manage devices (console servers, dedicated mgmt network, cellular) that still works when the production network is down.
🔐TLSTAP TO FLIP
Transport Layer Security — the encryption protocol behind HTTPS and many client-to-site VPNs (TCP 443). Provides confidentiality, integrity, and server authentication.

Lesson Content

At 2 a.m. during an outage, documentation is the difference between a ten-minute fix and an all-night archaeology dig. The N10-009 blueprint names these artifacts:

Lifecycle management — from purchase to shredder

  • EOL vs. EOS — End of Life: no longer sold. End of Support: no longer patched. EOL gear is a budget item; EOS gear is a security liability — every new vulnerability is permanent.
  • Firmware & patch management — track versions in the asset inventory, test patches outside production, deploy in maintenance windows. Unpatched network gear is the quiet way in.
  • Decommissioning — retirement is a process: remove from monitoring and inventory, revoke credentials and certificates, and sanitize or destroy storage — configs hold passwords, SNMP strings, and VPN keys.

Change management — how production gets modified

The point of change management is that no one is smart enough to safely improvise on production. The standard flow:

① Request (what, why, risk, affected systems) → ② Approval by a change advisory board (CAB) → ③ Maintenance window scheduled for low impact → ④ Implementation with a tested rollback plan standing by → ⑤ Validation & documentation — update the diagrams, inventory, and baselines from Section 1 so reality and records still match.

🎓Exam note: "A firmware upgrade failed at 2 a.m. — what should have existed before the change began?" → the rollback plan. "Device no longer receives security patches" → end of support.

Baselines and golden configurations

A configuration baseline (golden config) is the approved, known-good configuration for a device class. Automated tools diff running configs against it nightly — drift means either an undocumented change or an intruder. A performance baseline does the same for metrics: you can't recognize "abnormal" CPU or bandwidth without a recorded "normal." Every device config gets backed up on schedule and before every change (Section 2's rollback plan is usually just "restore last night's config").

The four backup types — what each actually copies

⚠️The 3-2-1 rule: keep 3 copies of data, on 2 different media, with 1 off-site. A snapshot stored on the same array as the VM satisfies none of those — if the array dies, the "backup" dies with it.
🎓Exam note: The trade is always the same — incremental = fastest backup, slowest restore (the whole chain); differential = bigger nightly, fastest restore (two sets). "Image of a VM before an upgrade" = snapshot.

SNMP — the management workhorse

The Simple Network Management Protocol (UDP 161/162) lets a network management station (NMS) read — and optionally write — device data organized in a MIB (Management Information Base) and addressed by OIDs. Two directions, one crucial difference:

Version matters: SNMPv2c "secures" everything with a plaintext community string; SNMPv3 adds real authentication and encryption and is the only version that belongs on a modern network.

Three altitudes of traffic visibility

  • SNMP counters — 30,000 ft: how much traffic, per interface. ("G0/1 is at 90%.")
  • Flow data (NetFlow/IPFIX/sFlow) — 10,000 ft: who is talking to whom — source/destination, ports, bytes, duration. The network's phone bill. ("One workstation is sending 40 GB to an unknown host.")
  • Packet capture — ground level: every byte of the conversation, via Wireshark/tcpdump. On a switched network the analyzer sees nothing until you configure port mirroring (SPAN) — the switch copies chosen ports' traffic to the analyzer's port.

Log aggregation & APIs

Syslog (UDP 514) ships every device's logs to one collector, stamped with severities 0 (emergency) through 7 (debug) — centralized, searchable, and correlated by NTP-synced timestamps (Lesson 5 pays off). SIEM platforms sit on top to alert on patterns. Modern gear also exposes REST APIs, letting monitoring dashboards and automation query structured JSON instead of screen-scraping CLI output.

🎓Exam note: Match the question to the altitude — utilization trend = SNMP; "who's consuming the bandwidth" = flow data; "what exactly is in the packets" = capture + port mirroring. Unsolicited device alert = trap. Severity 0 is emergency, 7 is debug.

Active-active vs. active-passive

Active-passive: the standby waits, taking over on failure — simple, but half your hardware idles (Lesson 4's VRRP is exactly this pattern at gateway scale). Active-active: all sites/nodes serve traffic simultaneously — full utilization and instant "failover" (survivors just absorb the load), at the price of synchronization complexity.

Testing — a plan you haven't tested is a rumor

  • Tabletop exercise — the team walks through the plan verbally against a scenario. Cheap, zero risk, finds gaps in roles and assumptions.
  • Failover test — actually cut over to the DR site/standby and run production there. Expensive and disruptive — and the only proof the plan works.
🎓Exam note: RPO = data (backward), RTO = time (forward). "Empty facility with power and cooling" = cold. "All nodes serving simultaneously" = active-active. "Discussion-based walkthrough" = tabletop.

A Virtual Private Network builds an encrypted tunnel across an untrusted network (the internet), so remote traffic travels as if it were on the private LAN. The original packet is encapsulated — encrypted and wrapped inside a new outer packet addressed to the VPN gateway. Two architectures, one big policy choice:

  • Site-to-site — two VPN gateways (routers/firewalls) maintain an always-on tunnel, typically IPsec. Whole offices talk to each other; end hosts need no VPN software and don't even know it's there.
  • Client-to-site (remote access) — an individual device runs VPN client software and tunnels to the corporate concentrator, commonly over TLS (TCP 443) so it works from any hotel network. This is where the tunneling policy applies:
    • Full tunnel — all the client's traffic goes through HQ, even Netflix. Maximum inspection and control; maximum load on the corporate internet link.
    • Split tunnel — only corporate-bound traffic uses the tunnel; everything else exits the local network directly. Efficient — but general browsing bypasses HQ's security stack.

Jump box (jump host / bastion)

Instead of letting fifty admin workstations SSH to every switch, all administration funnels through one hardened, monitored, logged intermediary. Firewall rules allow management access only from the jump box; admins authenticate to it (with MFA), and every session is recorded. One controlled doorway beats fifty unlocked ones — and one audit trail answers "who touched that router?"

In-band vs. out-of-band management

  • In-band — management traffic (SSH, SNMP, HTTPS) rides the same production network as user data. Convenient, free — and useless during the exact outages you need it most: if the network is down, so is your way in.
  • Out-of-band (OOB) — a separate physical path: console/serial servers, a dedicated management network on its own switches, or a cellular modem on the router. When production melts at 2 a.m., OOB is how you reach the console without driving to the data center.
💡How it fits together: the remote admin connects by client-to-site VPN (Section 6), lands on the jump box, and crosses to the OOB management network. Three layers, one auditable path — each layer useless to an attacker without the previous one.
🎓Exam note: "Manage the router when the WAN link is down" = out-of-band (console server / cellular). "Single hardened host that all administrative sessions pass through" = jump box.

Knowledge Check

Q1. A technician needs to know which switch port serves the wall jack in Room 214. Which document?
Rationale: Cable maps trace each run jack → patch panel port → switch port. Logical diagrams show subnets and flows, not physical terminations.
Q2. A vendor announces a switch model will stop receiving security patches next year. That date is the device's…
Rationale: EOL = no longer sold; EOS = no longer patched. EOS is the security deadline — every vulnerability found afterward is permanent.
Q3. Backups run: full on Sunday, incrementals Monday–Saturday. The server dies Thursday afternoon. What does the restore require?
Rationale: Each incremental holds only the changes since the previous backup, so the whole chain must be replayed in sequence. That restore-time cost is the price of tiny, fast nightly backups — a differential scheme would need just two sets.
Q4. The NOC must be alerted the instant an interface goes down — not at the next polling cycle. Configure…
Rationale: Traps are unsolicited, event-driven messages (UDP 162) sent the moment something happens. Polling waits for the schedule; flow data and mirroring answer different questions entirely.
Q5. Security wants to know which internal host is uploading gigabytes to an unknown internet address — without capturing packet contents. Best tool?
Rationale: Flow data is the network's phone bill — who talked to whom, ports, bytes, duration — exactly the "which host, which destination, how much" question, with no payload capture.
Q6. A company can tolerate losing at most 15 minutes of data in a disaster. That requirement is its…
Rationale: RPO measures backward — acceptable data loss between the last good copy and the disaster. RTO measures forward — acceptable downtime until restoration. A 15-minute RPO demands near-continuous replication.
Q7. Remote employees complain the corporate internet link is saturated because all their streaming traffic passes through HQ. Which change fixes this?
Rationale: Full tunnel hairpins everything through HQ. Split tunneling sends only corporate destinations through the tunnel and lets streaming exit locally — trading HQ inspection of that traffic for bandwidth relief.
Q8. The WAN link to a branch is down and the router is unreachable over the network. Which capability lets an engineer reach its console anyway?
Rationale: In-band rides the production network — the thing that's down. OOB is a separate physical path (console/serial, dedicated mgmt network, cellular) built precisely for this moment.
