What an IT specialist needs to know about router-based firewalls

A router-based firewall decides which packets may cross between networks you trust and networks you don't. Your job is to turn a business policy into rules, apply them in the right place, and prove they work. Read this briefing, then complete Parts A and B and the test plan.

Packet filtering

Access control lists judge each packet on its own: addresses, protocol, and ports. Fast and simple, but they cannot tell a real reply from a forged one. Part A builds three ACLs.

Stateful inspection

A zone-based policy firewall remembers every connection started from a trusted zone and admits only its genuine replies. Part B builds three zones and four policies.

Testing

The same 15 tests run in three phases: no filtering, ACLs only, and full firewall. Predict each result first. The test plan records what actually happens.

Knowledge an IT specialist needs

Firewall concepts

  • Firewall types: stateless packet filters, stateful firewalls, and next-generation firewalls (application awareness, IPS, URL filtering).
  • Default deny and least privilege: permit only what the business needs. Every ACL ends with an invisible deny any.
  • Session tables: a stateful firewall tracks source, destination, ports, and TCP state, so replies need no rule of their own.
  • Zones and trust levels: INSIDE (trusted), DMZ (public servers, semi-trusted), OUTSIDE (untrusted), and the router's own self zone.
  • Defense in depth: stateless anti-spoofing filters and stateful inspection work together.

Supporting network knowledge

  • TCP/IP behavior: the three-way handshake, TCP flags (SYN, ACK, RST), and why UDP and ICMP have no connections.
  • Ports: DNS 53, HTTP 80, HTTPS 443, SSH 22, Telnet 23, SMB 445, NetBIOS 137 to 139, DHCP 67/68.
  • Wildcard masks: the inverse of a subnet mask. A /24 is 0.0.0.255; one host is host or 0.0.0.0.
  • NAT and PAT: inside local vs. inside global addresses, and whether a rule sees the address before or after translation.
  • Address ranges to block: RFC 1918 private space, loopback, multicast, and your own public addresses arriving from outside (BCP 38).
Skills an IT specialist must demonstrate

Plan

  • Turn a business requirement into a zone policy table.
  • Choose standard or extended ACLs, and place each one on the correct interface and direction.
  • Decide what the router itself must accept (management, routing, replies).

Configure

  • Restrict device management to one admin host with SSH and an access-class.
  • Write named extended ACLs for egress and ingress filtering.
  • Build zones, class-maps, policy-maps, and zone-pairs, then assign interfaces to zones.

Test and troubleshoot

  • Run a written test plan from inside, DMZ, and outside hosts. Compare predicted and actual results.
  • Read ACL hit counters, firewall session tables, and drop logs to find the rule behind each result.
  • Change one thing at a time, then re-run the failed test.

Maintain

  • Document every rule with its business reason. Use remark lines.
  • Review logs for repeated drops, and back up configurations before every change.
  • Remove rules nobody can justify.

Order of operations on R1-SOHO

Knowing what the router checks, and in which order, tells you which address each rule must use.

  1. Inbound ACL on the interface where the packet arrives. Outside packets still carry public addresses, such as 203.0.113.3.
  2. Destination NAT for packets from outside. 203.0.113.3 becomes the web server's real address, 192.168.20.10.
  3. Routing picks the exit interface.
  4. Zone-based firewall checks the zone-pair from the arrival zone to the exit zone. It sees real (inside) addresses.
  5. Source NAT (PAT) for packets leaving to the internet, then the outbound ACL, if any.
  6. Replies run the same checks in reverse. An inspected session lets the reply past the firewall automatically.

Part B: Stateful firewall with zone-based policy

The ACLs from Part A guess at which packets are replies. Replace that guesswork with a session table. Build the zones and policies first, and assign interfaces to zones last, because traffic between a zoned and an unzoned interface is dropped.

There is deliberately no zone-pair into INSIDE. With no zone-pair, the firewall denies the traffic; that absence is the rule.

Challenge (bonus): protect the router itself

After Part B, ATTACKER can still ping 203.0.113.2, because traffic to the router itself is in the self zone, which no zone-pair covers. Add an OUTSIDE-to-self zone-pair that drops unsolicited traffic to the router but still lets R1-SOHO's own pings to the ISP (203.0.113.1) get their replies. Hint: use the pass action, and remember that it works in one direction only.
