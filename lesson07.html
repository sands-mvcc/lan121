Network Security — Threats, Vulnerabilities & Countermeasures

Everything you've built in six lessons is a target. This lesson organizes defense the way professionals do: foundations first — the CIA triad, encryption in transit and at rest, PKI and certificates; then identity and access management — authentication, MFA, SSO, Kerberos, RADIUS, TACACS+, LDAP, and SAML, governed by least privilege and role-based access; then the attacks themselves — DoS/DDoS, VLAN hopping, MAC flooding, ARP and DNS poisoning, rogue devices and evil twins, on-path attacks, social engineering, and malware; and finally the countermeasures — device hardening, NAC with 802.1X and port security, firewalls, IDS/IPS, ACLs, security zones, deception technologies, and segmentation for IoT, SCADA/OT, guest, and BYOD networks, wrapped in audits and regulatory compliance. Eleven NCyTE interactive activities are embedded where they teach best. Exam 4 covers this domain.

Key Terms & Concepts

🛡️CIA TriadTAP TO FLIP
The three goals of security: Confidentiality (only authorized eyes), Integrity (unaltered data), Availability (there when needed). Every control serves at least one.
📜PKITAP TO FLIP
Public Key Infrastructure — the system of Certificate Authorities, digital certificates, and key pairs that lets strangers verify identity and exchange keys. The trust behind HTTPS.
🔢MFATAP TO FLIP
Multi-Factor Authentication — proof from at least two categories: something you KNOW (password), HAVE (token/phone), or ARE (biometric). Two passwords ≠ MFA.
🎫SSOTAP TO FLIP
Single Sign-On — authenticate once, access many systems. Delivered by protocols like Kerberos inside the LAN and SAML across web applications.
🐕KerberosTAP TO FLIP
Ticket-based authentication protocol (the three heads: client, service, KDC). Passwords never cross the wire — time-stamped encrypted tickets do. Active Directory's engine.
📻RADIUSTAP TO FLIP
The AAA protocol (authentication, authorization, accounting) for network access — Wi-Fi, VPN, 802.1X. UDP 1812/1813; encrypts only the password field.
🖥️TACACS+TAP TO FLIP
Cisco's AAA protocol for DEVICE administration — TCP 49, encrypts the whole payload, and separates authentication from authorization for per-command control.
🚦802.1XTAP TO FLIP
Port-based network access control: the supplicant (device) proves itself through the authenticator (switch/AP) to the authentication server (RADIUS) before the port passes traffic.
🛂NACTAP TO FLIP
Network Access Control — admits devices based on identity AND posture (patched? antivirus?), then assigns the right VLAN. 802.1X is its enforcement backbone.
🔌Port SecurityTAP TO FLIP
Switch feature limiting which/how many MAC addresses a port accepts; violations can shut the port down. Kills MAC flooding and casual rogue devices.
👯Evil TwinTAP TO FLIP
A rogue access point broadcasting a legitimate network's SSID so victims connect through the attacker — on-path position achieved by imitation.
☠️ARP PoisoningTAP TO FLIP
Forged ARP replies convince hosts the attacker's MAC belongs to the gateway's IP — traffic detours through the attacker. ARP has no authentication; it believes anyone.
🕳️On-Path AttackTAP TO FLIP
Formerly "man-in-the-middle" — the attacker relays (and can read or alter) traffic between two parties who believe they're talking directly. ARP poisoning and evil twins are the entry moves.
🦘VLAN HoppingTAP TO FLIP
Escaping your VLAN via switch-spoofing (negotiating a trunk with DTP) or double-tagging (nested 802.1Q tags). Defense: disable DTP, hard-code access ports, move the native VLAN.
🍯HoneypotTAP TO FLIP
A decoy system built to be attacked — no legitimate user touches it, so every interaction is an alarm and an intelligence feed. A honeynet is a whole decoy network.
🏰Screened SubnetTAP TO FLIP
The zone (formerly DMZ) between two firewall layers where public-facing servers live — the internet can reach them, but a compromise there still can't reach the internal LAN directly.
📃ACLTAP TO FLIP
Access Control List — ordered permit/deny rules evaluated top-down, first match wins, with an implicit "deny all" at the end. Traffic law on routers and switches.
🏭SCADATAP TO FLIP
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition — the industrial control systems running utilities and factories. Often unpatchable and safety-critical: isolate OT from IT ruthlessly.

Lesson Content

Every security control in this lesson exists to defend one or more of three properties:

Encryption — in transit and at rest

  • In transit — data moving across the network: TLS for web and mail, IPsec/TLS VPN tunnels (Lesson 6), WPA3 on wireless, SSH instead of Telnet. Defeats eavesdropping and on-path reading.
  • At rest — data sitting on storage: full-disk encryption (BitLocker/LUKS), encrypted databases and backups, self-encrypting drives. Defeats theft of the physical media — a stolen encrypted laptop is a hardware loss, not a breach.

PKI and certificates — trust between strangers

Symmetric encryption is fast but both sides need the same key — how do you share it with a server you've never met? Public Key Infrastructure: every subject holds a key pair (public + private), and a trusted Certificate Authority (CA) signs a digital certificate binding the public key to an identity. Your browser trusts the CA, the CA vouches for the server, and the two sides use the public key to agree on a fast symmetric session key. Key management is the discipline around it — generating, distributing, rotating, storing, and revoking keys; the strongest algorithm is worthless if the private key leaks or an expired certificate goes unnoticed (recall from Lesson 5 why accurate time matters here).

Authentication proves identity; authorization grants rights; accounting records what happened — AAA. MFA strengthens the first A by requiring factors from different categories: something you know, have, or are (plus somewhere you are — geofencing restricts access by physical location). SSO means one authentication unlocks many systems.

Governing principles

  • Least privilege — every account gets the minimum rights the job requires, nothing more. The blast radius of any compromise shrinks to match.
  • Role-based access control (RBAC) — rights attach to roles (Instructor, Help Desk, Student), and people inherit from role membership. Onboarding and offboarding become one group change instead of fifty permission edits.

Network & Layer 2 attacks

  • DoS / DDoS — exhaust a service's capacity (bandwidth, connections, CPU) so legitimate users can't get in; DDoS does it from thousands of botnet machines at once. Pure availability attack.
  • MAC flooding — blast a switch with fake source MACs until its MAC table overflows and it fails open, flooding all traffic like a hub the attacker can sniff.
  • VLAN hopping — escape segmentation by switch spoofing (negotiating a trunk via DTP) or double tagging (nested 802.1Q tags that survive the first strip). Lesson 3's trunking knowledge, weaponized.
  • ARP poisoning/spoofing — ARP (Lesson 5) has zero authentication, so forged replies rewrite victims' caches: "the gateway's IP is at MY MAC." Traffic now detours through the attacker — the classic on-path setup:
  • DNS poisoning — corrupt a resolver's cache so a good name returns the attacker's address; whole populations get redirected (the integrity problem DNSSEC from Lesson 5 exists to solve).
  • Rogue devices & evil twins — unauthorized hardware on your network: a rogue DHCP server handing out attacker gateways, a rogue AP in a cubicle, or an evil twin broadcasting your SSID so victims connect through it.
  • On-path attack — the destination position of ARP poisoning and evil twins: the attacker silently relays between two parties, reading or altering everything unencrypted.

The human layer

  • Phishing (and spear phishing/vishing/smishing) — fraudulent messages harvesting credentials or delivering malware. Still the #1 initial access vector.
  • Tailgating — following an authorized person through a secured door; shoulder surfing — reading credentials over someone's shoulder. Physical controls exist because these work.
  • Malware — viruses, worms, trojans, and the headline act, ransomware: encrypt the victim's data, sell back the key — which is why Lesson 6's offline, tested backups are a security control, not just an ops chore.

Device hardening — shrinking the attack surface

Every device ships friendly and dies hard: change default credentials, disable unused services and ports, replace insecure protocols (Telnet→SSH, HTTP→HTTPS, SNMPv2c→SNMPv3), keep firmware patched (Lesson 6's lifecycle management), and protect the management plane — ACL-restricted access, ideally out-of-band. Then verify against the configuration baseline so hardening survives the next change.

NAC and 802.1X — authenticate the device before the port works

Port security (below) trusts MAC addresses; 802.1X trusts credentials. Until authentication succeeds, the switch port passes nothing but the authentication conversation itself:

Full NAC adds posture: is the device patched, encrypted, running AV? Pass → production VLAN; fail → quarantine VLAN for remediation. This is how BYOD gets admitted without being trusted.

Port security — the Layer 2 seatbelt

Where 802.1X isn't practical (printers, cameras), port security limits a port to specific or a maximum number of MAC addresses (sticky-learned), with a violation action — typically shutdown (err-disabled). It blunts MAC flooding and casual rogue devices; pair with DHCP snooping and dynamic ARP inspection to counter rogue DHCP servers and ARP poisoning at the switch itself.

Wireless security

Use WPA3 (or WPA2 minimum) — never WEP/open; WPA3-Enterprise puts 802.1X/RADIUS behind every association, so there's no shared passphrase to leak. Combine with a segmented guest SSID, and rogue-AP detection on the WLC (Lesson 3) to hunt evil twins.

Firewalls & ACLs

A firewall enforces policy between zones of different trust. Modern stateful firewalls track connections (a reply is only allowed if a matching outbound request exists); next-generation firewalls add application awareness and content filtering (block categories, scan payloads). The underlying grammar everywhere is the ACL: ordered permit/deny rules, evaluated top-down, first match wins, ending in an implicit deny all — so rule order is itself a security control.

Physical security

Every logical control assumes the attacker can't touch the hardware. Badge readers and locks (with anti-tailgating vestibules where it matters), cameras for deterrence and evidence, locked racks (Lesson 3), and clean media disposal (Lesson 6's decommissioning). Tailgating and shoulder surfing from Section 3 are precisely the attacks this layer answers.

Deception technologies

A honeypot is a deliberately attractive decoy — a fake server with tempting weaknesses. No legitimate user ever touches it, so every interaction is a high-confidence alarm, and the attacker's techniques become free threat intelligence while they waste time in the sandbox. A honeynet scales the idea to an entire decoy network.

Audits & regulatory compliance

  • Audits — independent verification that controls exist and work: configs vs. baselines, access reviews against least privilege, log evidence (Lessons 5–6 built the audit trail).
  • PCI DSS — contractual rules for anyone handling payment cards: segment the cardholder-data environment, encrypt, restrict, log.
  • GDPR — EU law protecting personal data, with real fines and breach-notification clocks.
  • Data locality — laws dictating where data may physically reside; your cloud region choice is a compliance decision, not just a latency one.
🎓Exam note: "Decoy system whose only purpose is to be attacked and studied" = honeypot. Payment cards = PCI DSS; EU personal data = GDPR; "data must remain in-country" = data locality.

Knowledge Check

Q1. A DDoS attack that takes a web service offline primarily violates which element of the CIA triad?
Rationale: Nothing was read (confidentiality) or altered (integrity) — the service simply isn't there when legitimate users need it. DoS/DDoS is the textbook availability attack.
Q2. Which protocol should manage administrator logins TO the routers and switches themselves, with full-payload encryption and per-command authorization?
Rationale: TACACS+ (TCP 49) is built for device administration: full encryption and separated authorization for per-command control. RADIUS is for network access (Wi-Fi/VPN/802.1X) and encrypts only the password.
Q3. Users report reaching a convincing fake of a banking site even though they typed the correct URL. DNS on the resolver checks out as compromised. What happened?
Rationale: Correct name → wrong address is the DNS poisoning signature; every client of that resolver is redirected. DNSSEC's signed records (Lesson 5) exist to make exactly this forgery detectable.
Q4. In 802.1X, what role does the switch play?
Rationale: Three roles: supplicant (client device), authenticator (switch/AP — the gatekeeper in the middle), authentication server (RADIUS — the decision maker). Until ACCEPT, the port passes only authentication traffic.
Q5. Security wants malicious traffic BLOCKED in real time, accepting the risk that a false positive could interrupt legitimate traffic. Deploy…
Rationale: Blocking requires being inline — that's the IPS. An IDS watches a copy of traffic and can only alert; the stated false-positive trade-off is exactly the IPS trade-off.
Q6. A new public-facing web server must be reachable from the internet. Per security zone design, where does it belong?
Rationale: The screened subnet (DMZ) exists for exactly this: the internet can reach the server, but if it's compromised, a second firewall still stands between the attacker and the internal LAN.
Q7. Hundreds of factory PLCs run an OS the vendor stopped patching years ago, and they control physical machinery. Best available control?
Rationale: SCADA/OT gear often can't be patched or run agents — so the network becomes the control: ruthless segmentation plus one audited doorway (Lesson 6's jump box). What can't be hardened must be isolated.
Q8. The SOC notices logins on a server that hosts no real services and that no employee should ever touch. What is this system, and what does the activity mean?
Rationale: A honeypot's value is that it has zero legitimate purpose: no false-positive debate, every touch is signal. Meanwhile the attacker wastes time and reveals techniques in a controlled sandbox.
