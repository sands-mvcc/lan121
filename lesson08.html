Network Troubleshooting — Methodology, Tools & Performance

This is the domain the whole course was building toward — and at 24%, the biggest slice of the N10-009 exam. Troubleshooting is where knowledge becomes skill: the CompTIA seven-step methodology that replaces guesswork with process; Layer 1 diagnosis — cable faults, interface error counters, port states, PoE budgets, and transceivers; service failures in DNS, DHCP, NTP, authentication, VPN, and NAT that call back everything from Lessons 4 through 7; performance problems — latency, jitter, packet loss, congestion, and the wireless gremlins of RSSI, channel overlap, and hidden nodes; and the toolbag itself, from ping and dig to TDRs and Wi-Fi analyzers, with an interactive drill to sharpen your tool selection. Then: the Final Exam and the capstone lab.

🎯

Learning Objectives

  1. Apply the CompTIA seven-step troubleshooting methodology to a reported problem, in order, from identification through documentation. BLOOM: APPLY
  2. Select a hypothesis strategy — top-down, bottom-up, or divide and conquer — appropriate to the symptoms. BLOOM: ANALYZE
  3. Diagnose cabling and physical interface problems from symptoms and error counters: attenuation, crosstalk, EMI/RFI, CRC errors, runts/giants, duplex mismatch, port states, PoE budget, and transceiver faults. BLOOM: ANALYZE
  4. Trace service failures — DNS, DHCP, NTP, authentication, VPN, and NAT — to their root cause using the behavior each failure produces. BLOOM: ANALYZE
  5. Differentiate the performance metrics — throughput, latency, jitter, and packet loss — and identify causes of congestion and wireless degradation (RSSI, SNR, channel overlap, hidden node, roaming). BLOOM: ANALYZE
  6. Choose the correct software or hardware tool for a diagnostic question and interpret its output. BLOOM: EVALUATE
  7. Document a completed incident — problem, root cause, actions, resolution, and lessons learned — so the next technician starts ahead. BLOOM: CREATE

Key Terms & Concepts

🎯Root Cause AnalysisTAP TO FLIP
Digging past the symptom to the underlying cause — restarting a service clears the symptom; finding why it crashed prevents the repeat at 3 a.m.
📈EscalationTAP TO FLIP
Handing the problem to someone with more authority, access, or expertise — a defined step in the methodology, not an admission of failure.
↩️Rollback PlanTAP TO FLIP
The tested path back to the pre-change state, prepared BEFORE implementing a fix — Lesson 6's change management rule, applied under pressure.
↕️Top-Down / Bottom-UpTAP TO FLIP
Walking the OSI model from Layer 7 downward (start at the app) or Layer 1 upward (start at the cable) until you find the broken layer.
✂️Divide & ConquerTAP TO FLIP
Start at a middle layer (can you ping?) and let the result cut the problem space in half — success means look up the stack, failure means look down.
📉AttenuationTAP TO FLIP
Signal weakening over distance — the reason copper runs stop at 100 m. Symptoms: intermittent or failed links on long runs.
🗣️CrosstalkTAP TO FLIP
Signal bleeding between adjacent wire pairs — often from sloppy termination that untwisted too much pair. Better cable categories and STP fight it.
⚡EMI / RFITAP TO FLIP
Electromagnetic / radio-frequency interference from motors, fluorescent lights, and transmitters corrupting signals — why data cable avoids power runs, and why shielded (STP) exists.
🧮CRC ErrorTAP TO FLIP
A frame arrived but its checksum didn't match — it was damaged in transit. Rising CRC counters point at bad cable, interference, or duplex mismatch.
🐜Runt / GiantTAP TO FLIP
Frames below 64 bytes (runts — often collision fragments) or above the MTU (giants — often an MTU/jumbo mismatch from Lesson 3).
🤝Duplex MismatchTAP TO FLIP
One side full duplex, the other half — the link works but crawls, logging late collisions and CRC errors. The classic "up but terrible" diagnosis.
🔋PoE BudgetTAP TO FLIP
The total watts a switch can deliver across all ports (802.3af 15.4W · at 30W · bt 60–90W per port). Exceed it and new devices simply don't power on.
🚩APIPATAP TO FLIP
169.254.x.x — the address a client self-assigns when DHCP never answered. Not the problem; the symptom that says "investigate DHCP."
🐢LatencyTAP TO FLIP
Time for a packet to make the trip — from distance, congested queues, firewall inspection, VPN encryption. Measured by ping's round-trip time.
📳JitterTAP TO FLIP
VARIATION in latency — packets arriving at uneven intervals. The metric that ruins VoIP and video; QoS exists largely to control it.
📶RSSI / SNRTAP TO FLIP
Received signal strength (closer to 0 dBm is stronger; −67 good, −85 unusable) and signal-to-noise ratio — a strong signal in a noisy room can still be an unusable one.
📡TDR / OTDRTAP TO FLIP
Reflectometers that send a pulse down copper (TDR) or fiber (OTDR) and time the echo — reporting the DISTANCE to the break, short, or bad splice.
🔁Loopback AdapterTAP TO FLIP
A plug that wires TX straight to RX so a port tests itself — isolating "is the interface bad?" from "is the cable or far end bad?"

Lesson Content

Every technician has watched someone "fix" a network by rebooting things until the symptom disappeared — leaving the cause alive and the next outage scheduled. The CompTIA methodology exists so that never has to be you. The exam tests the steps in order:

Three ways to hunt a theory (step ②)

  • Top-down — start at Layer 7 and descend: app config, then DNS, then IP, then link. Natural when "one application is broken but the network seems fine."
  • Bottom-up — start at Layer 1 and climb: link lights, cable, interface counters, IP, service. Natural when "nothing works at all."
  • Divide and conquer — jump to the middle and let one test halve the search: ping 8.8.8.8 works? The problem is above Layer 3 — probably DNS or the app. Fails? Look down at addressing, gateway, link.
⚠️Two rules the exam loves: if you must escalate, it happens when a theory can't be confirmed (step ③) or the fix exceeds your authority (step ⑤). And the job is not done at "it works" — it's done at step ⑦, when the documentation is written. Skip it and the next tech starts from zero.

Copper problems

  • Attenuation — signal fades with distance; twisted-pair runs cap at 100 m. Symptom: the far cubicle's link flaps or trains down to a lower speed.
  • Crosstalk — pairs interfering with each other, usually from terminations that untwisted too much wire, or under-spec cable (Cat5e pushed to 10G).
  • EMI/RFI — motors, fluorescent ballasts, and elevators inducing noise. Fix: reroute away from power, or use shielded (STP) cable.
  • Termination & damage — opens, shorts, split pairs, and the wrong cable for the job (flat patch cable in a 90 m horizontal run).

Fiber problems

Dirty connectors (the #1 fiber issue — clean before you condemn), bend radius violations leaking light, TX/RX reversed (dead link, fixed by flipping the pair), and mismatches: single-mode gear on multimode fiber, or transceivers on different wavelengths.

Port states tell the rest of the story: up/up (healthy), down/down (physical problem — cable, far end, transceiver), administratively down (someone typed shutdown — configuration, not hardware), error-disabled (the switch punished a violation — port security from Lesson 7, BPDU Guard from Lesson 3), suspended (usually an LACP/EtherChannel misconfig, Lesson 3).

PoE — when the device won't even power on

StandardNamePer-port power
802.3afPoE15.4 W
802.3atPoE+30 W
802.3btPoE++ (Type 3/4)60 / 90–100 W

Two classic failures: the device needs more than the port's standard supplies (a PTZ camera on an 802.3af port), or the switch's total power budget is exhausted — the first twenty phones power up and number twenty-one stays dark even though the port is fine.

Transceivers: SFP (1G), SFP+ (10G), QSFP (40G+) — a mismatch in speed, wavelength, or fiber type between the two ends means no light, no link. Verify with the datasheet, or swap in a known-good pair.

🎓Exam note: Late collisions + CRC on a link that's "up" = duplex mismatch. Port shows err-disabled = a protection feature fired, check port security/BPDU Guard. New PoE device won't power = check the standard AND the remaining budget.

💡The four-ping ladder from Lesson 5 still rules: loopback → gateway → 8.8.8.8 → google.com. Where the ladder breaks names the layer: last rung only = DNS; third rung = routing/NAT; second = LAN; first = the host itself.

Outages are easy — something is down. Performance problems are subtler: everything is up, and everyone is miserable. Four metrics separate the complaints:

  • Throughput — how much data actually moves. Killed by saturation and oversubscription (forty 1G users funneled through one 1G uplink), misapplied traffic shaping, or a rogue broadcast storm (Lesson 3's STP failure) consuming the segment.
  • Latency — delay from distance, congested router queues, deep firewall inspection, VPN encryption overhead. Batch transfers shrug; interactive apps suffer.
  • Jitter — variable latency. Buffers can hide a little; VoIP and video die by it — the reason QoS gives real-time traffic the express lane.
  • Packet loss — from interface errors (Section 2), congestion drops, or wireless interference. TCP retransmits (slowly); real-time UDP just glitches.
  • Device resources — high CPU/memory or buffer exhaustion on a switch/router makes a healthy link perform like a sick one. Check the box, not just the wire — against the baseline from Lesson 6.

Wireless performance gremlins

  • Low RSSI / poor SNR — weak signal or strong noise; −67 dBm is a common design floor for voice/video.
  • Channel overlap / co-channel interference — neighbors off the 1/6/11 plan (Lesson 3's channel widget pays off here).
  • Hidden node — two clients both hear the AP but not each other, so they collide at it. RTS/CTS mitigates.
  • Roaming problems — sticky clients clinging to a distant AP; fix with power tuning, minimum data rates, and band steering (Lesson 3).
🎓Exam note: Choppy VoIP with everything else fine = jitter → QoS. Slow every afternoon at the same hour = congestion/oversubscription — check utilization against the baseline. Wireless fine near the AP, awful by the window = RSSI/coverage, bring the Wi-Fi analyzer.

Hardware tools

Cable tester — wiremap: opens, shorts, split/crossed pairs. Tone generator & probe — find WHICH cable in the bundle this jack is. Loopback plug — make a port test itself. TDR/OTDR — distance to the fault on copper/fiber. Punch-down tool & crimper — terminate panels and ends. Multimeter — voltage/continuity (PoE checks). Wi-Fi analyzer — RSSI, SNR, channel occupancy; the wireless survey instrument from Lesson 6's documentation.

Monitoring & packet analysis

The Lesson 6 stack is also a troubleshooting stack: syslog for what devices said, SNMP for counters and traps, NetFlow for who's talking, and packet capture (Wireshark + port mirroring) for ground truth. In captures, know what healthy looks like — the TCP three-way handshake (SYN → SYN/ACK → ACK), DORA, DNS query/answer pairs, ARP request/reply, 802.1Q tags — so the broken version jumps out (a lonely SYN retransmitting = something's dropping or filtering; an immediate RST = the port is closed or refused).

Knowledge Check

Q1. A user reports "the internet is down." Per the methodology, what comes FIRST?
Rationale: Step ① is always identification: gather information, interview, determine recent changes, duplicate if possible. Acting before understanding is exactly the guesswork the methodology exists to prevent.
Q2. A tech runs ping 8.8.8.8 as their very first test, planning to look up the stack if it succeeds and down if it fails. Which strategy is this?
Rationale: Starting at a middle layer and letting one result halve the search space is divide and conquer. Top-down starts at the application; bottom-up starts at the cable.
Q3. An interface shows climbing CRC errors and late collisions, and users say the connection is "up but painfully slow." Most likely cause?
Rationale: Late collisions + CRC + terrible throughput on an "up" link is the duplex mismatch signature you first met in Lesson 3 — one side full, one side half. Set both ends identically.
Q4. Twenty IP phones power up fine; the twenty-first, on a known-good port, gets link but no power. Best explanation?
Rationale: Per-port capability isn't the whole story — the switch has a total wattage budget across all ports. When it's spent, additional powered devices stay dark even on healthy ports.
Q5. A workstation can ping 8.8.8.8 but cannot reach any website by name. Which tool most directly investigates the failing layer?
Rationale: IP connectivity works; name resolution doesn't — that's DNS. nslookup/dig shows exactly what the resolver returns (or doesn't). The four-ping ladder broke on its last rung.
Q6. Users on video calls report robotic, garbled audio, though file downloads are fast. Which metric is the culprit, and which control addresses it?
Rationale: Fast downloads rule out raw bandwidth; uneven packet arrival (jitter) is what shreds real-time audio/video. QoS gives voice/video queues priority so their timing stays smooth.
Q7. A fiber uplink between buildings is dead. Which tool reports the distance to the break?
Rationale: The Optical Time Domain Reflectometer pulses light down the fiber and times the reflection — reporting how far away the break or bad splice is, so you dig in the right spot. TDR is its copper sibling.
Q8. The fix worked and the user confirms everything runs. Per the methodology, what remains before the ticket closes?
Rationale: Step ⑦. Documentation converts one technician's afternoon into the organization's knowledge — and it's the step the exam checks that you won't skip.
